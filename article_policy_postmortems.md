# Policy post-mortems: what did we know, and when did we know it?

**British government has a ritual for failure: a select committee report, an NAO study, eventually a public inquiry, each arriving years after the fact and none equipped to answer the only questions that prevent recurrence. This essay proposes a discipline borrowed from aviation and fiscal forecasting — the standing, rapid, blameless, *data-dated* policy post-mortem — sets out its four-question method, and demonstrates it end to end on England's SEND crisis, where the method shows the national collapse was predictable within 8% from mid-2017, that no one could have said which councils would fail, and that the delay in acting cost between £0.3bn and £1.2bn and 26,600 children's late plans.**

*Matt Sharpe, Oxford Internet Institute | August 2026*

---

## The ritual of failure

When a passenger aircraft crashes, investigators from the Air Accidents Investigation Branch are typically on site within hours. Their report — published, blameless by statute, focused on mechanism rather than culpability — feeds directly into airworthiness directives that change how every operator flies. The discipline is so routine that its extraordinary premise goes unremarked: the purpose of investigating failure is not to allocate blame but to make the same failure impossible.

When a British public service collapses, something different happens. The pressure builds for years in published statistics. A select committee holds hearings and issues a report with dozens of recommendations. The National Audit Office quantifies the damage. Eventually, if the failure is grave enough, a public inquiry is established — arriving perhaps a decade after the decisions it examines, legally constituted, adversarial in temperament, costing nine figures, and reporting long after everyone who made the relevant decisions has moved on. Somewhere in this process the phrase "lessons will be learned" is deployed, and the specific mechanism by which the next assumption-breach in the next demand-led system will trigger action before crisis is left exactly as undefined as before.

The gap is not a lack of retrospection. British government retrospects constantly. The gap is that none of the existing mechanisms is built to answer, quickly and quantitatively, the four questions that actually prevent recurrence:

1. **What did we know, and when did we know it?**
2. **What should we have done, and by when?**
3. **What did the delay cost?**
4. **What ensures the same breach cannot pass unactioned again?**

This essay proposes a discipline for answering them — the *policy post-mortem* — and demonstrates it on a live case.

---

## The precedents exist in fragments

The components of this discipline are not new. What is new is assembling them.

**The OBR's Forecast Evaluation Report** is the closest structural precedent. The Office for Budget Responsibility is required by statute to publish, at least annually, an assessment of its own past forecasts against outturn — to "identify, understand, and learn from differences." It is routine, expected, blame-free, and quantitative. But it exists only for macroeconomic and fiscal forecasts. There is no equivalent for the demand assumptions underneath statutory entitlement systems — special educational needs, adult social care, temporary accommodation, court and prison capacity — where the assumption-to-outturn gap is precisely what turns into human and fiscal crisis.

**Post-legislative scrutiny** formally exists: since 2008, departments are expected to submit a memorandum to the relevant select committee three to five years after an Act passes. In practice it is thin, qualitative, and unenforced. The Children and Families Act 2014 — whose Part 3 created the EHCP system — received its meaningful post-legislative scrutiny from a House of Lords committee in **2022**, eight years after Royal Assent and roughly five years after the Act's central planning assumption had visibly failed in the government's own published data. The committee concluded that government was "flying blind on data." It did not reconstruct what the data had shown at each date, did not cost the delay, and triggered nothing.

**Public inquiries** do ask "what was known and when" — it is their central forensic question, inherited from Howard Baker's formulation at Watergate. But they are engineered for accountability after catastrophe, not learning during drift: years long, lawyer-led, blame-oriented, and so expensive and exceptional that they cannot be the routine instrument.

**The NAO and Public Accounts Committee** produce rigorous retrospectives, and their SEND reports (2019, 2024) are essential. But value-for-money audit is not method-standardised post-mortem: it does not routinely reconstruct dated information sets, does not apply an explicit decision standard to what should have been done under uncertainty, and does not cost counterfactuals.

**The blameless-investigation model** itself has crossed one domain boundary already: the Healthcare Services Safety Investigations Body applies AAIB methods to clinical incidents, and Matthew Syed's *Black Box Thinking* made the general case a decade ago. Academic and journalistic post-mortems of government failure exist in narrative form — King and Crewe's *The Blunders of Our Governments* is the classic — but narrative history is not a reproducible method.

Each fragment exists. No mechanism combines them: **routine like the OBR report, blameless like the AAIB, quantitative like the NAO, and closed-loop — ending not in "lessons" but in published tripwires.**

## The method

A policy post-mortem answers the four questions with four corresponding disciplines. Everything below is implementable by a small analytical team with published data; the demonstration in the second half of this essay was produced by one analyst.

### 1. What did we know, and when? — the publication-vintage reconstruction

The first discipline is the hardest and the most commonly fudged: reconstruct the information set as it actually existed at each decision point, from the **original publications, dated to their release**, not from modern datasets truncated to look old.

The distinction matters more than it appears. A modern dataset truncated at 2017 tells you what the underlying administrative systems had *recorded* by 2017; a publication-vintage reconstruction tells you what anyone could *know* by 2017. These can diverge sharply — in the SEND demonstration below, an entire class of apparent early-warning signals (council-level tribunal appeal rates) turns out to have been first published in 2025, invalidating any claim that analysts at the time could have used them. A post-mortem that skips this discipline manufactures hindsight and will be — correctly — dismissed.

The output is twofold: a **publication audit** (for every series: what was published, at what geography, on what date, with what lag) and a **detection timeline** (for each release date: what the newly available data showed, measured against the planning assumptions of the day — in standard deviations, not adjectives).

Two fairness rules follow. First, the reconstruction must include the *exculpatory* evidence: the official explanations that were reasonable at the time, and the date at which each stopped being reasonable. Second, it must state what was **not** knowable — a post-mortem that finds everything foreseeable has usually assumed its conclusion.

### 2. What should we have done? — the decision standard under uncertainty

Hindsight bias is the standard objection to any retrospective, and the answer is to judge decisions against an explicit ex-ante standard rather than against the outcome. The right standard for demand-led systems with long supply lead times is **regret-sequencing under a stress test**:

- The question is never "should we have predicted the outcome?" — prevalence trends, demand surges, and cost spirals are genuinely hard to forecast. The question is "should the system have been tested against the scenarios its own data made plausible?" Banks cannot forecast recessions; they are still required to survive them.
- Interventions are sequenced by regret, not by certainty. Cheap, reversible actions with long lead times (training pipelines, audits, standards) are justified early, on confirmed trend alone, because their cost-if-wrong is trivial and their cost-of-delay compounds. Expensive, fixed actions (capital programmes) require the trend to be unambiguous — a threshold that must be stated, not gestured at (in the demonstration: two consecutive years of confirmed breach for low-regret actions; three, with the confounders eliminated, for capital).
- Lead times set the calendar. If an intervention takes four years to deliver, "wait until certain" is a decision to not act within the relevant horizon, and must be evaluated as such.

The output is a dated ledger: at each release date, which tier of response the evidence then supported, against what actually happened.

### 3. What did the delay cost? — counterfactual costing

The counterfactual is not "the crisis doesn't happen." It is narrower and more defensible: **the responses government eventually made, made at the date the evidence supported them.** The same capital programme, decided when its case was proven rather than four years later; the same workforce expansion, commissioned when the bottleneck was identifiable. This framing is deliberately hard to rebut — every element of the counterfactual is something government actually did, priced at what government actually paid.

Costing then requires declared assumptions, a sensitivity grid, and a conservative floor: a scope so cautious that accepting it concedes the argument (in the demonstration: a scenario in which *every* additional expensive placement is assumed clinically unavoidable, and only the change in placement mix is counted). Alongside the fiscal number, the human quantity — in SEND, children's statutory deadlines missed; in other systems, months on waiting lists, nights in B&Bs — stated in its own units, not monetised into invisibility.

### 4. How do we make sure? — assumptions with tripwires

The final discipline is the one that converts a report into a mechanism, and it operates *ex ante*, on the next policy rather than the last one:

- **Every major demand-led programme publishes its planning assumptions at launch** — the demand, cost, and throughput numbers the funding envelope depends on — as the 2014 SEND reforms' impact assessment did, to its credit.
- **Attached to each assumption is a published tripwire**: the deviation that, if observed for a defined period, automatically triggers a named tier of response. Not "we will monitor closely" — a number, a duration, and a consequence.
- **An annual assumptions-versus-outturn statement** — an OBR Forecast Evaluation Report for the demand-led state — reports every tracked system's position against its assumptions in one document. Breaches are visible to Parliament by default rather than discoverable by inquiry a decade later.
- **A breach sustained past its tripwire triggers the post-mortem automatically**, run by the four-question method, published within months, blameless by design: its subject is the assumption-to-action machinery, not the officials operating it.

The institutional home matters less than the obligation. The function could sit with the NAO, with an OBR-like unit, or as a Treasury requirement on departments with external audit — the Magenta Book already establishes evaluation doctrine that this extends from *interventions* to *planning assumptions*. What cannot work is the status quo, in which the existence of monitoring is assumed and the SEND record shows the assumption false: a 36-standard-deviation breach of a flagship reform's central planning assumption produced no institutional consequence for five years.

## The demonstration: England's SEND crisis

The method above was developed by applying it. The full analysis — code, data, publication audit, and three articles — is public ([github.com/Kali89/la-send-analysis](https://github.com/Kali89/la-send-analysis)); what follows is the compressed result of each discipline in turn.

**The reconstruction.** The 2014 reforms assumed roughly one-for-one conversion of SEN statements into the new EHC plans — a continuation of the statements-era trend of about +1% a year. Rebuilding the information set from the original May statistical releases: in May 2016 the first EHCP-era data showed a 6.7% jump, but the DfE's own release attributed it to the new 16–25 age range, and its age table supported that — school-age numbers actually fell that year. Mid-2016 was genuinely ambiguous; the clock should not start there. By **May 2017** every defence was failing on the department's own tables: caseload up 12.1% (36σ above the planning assumption), school-age growth positive, new plans 24% above any statements-era year. A one-line extrapolation of that release predicts the 2024 caseload within 7.5%; a year later, within 1.3%. A 36-cell stress test constructible entirely from mid-2017 publications contains everything that subsequently happened — caseload, late plans, independent-placement spend — with the actual outturn tracking the middle of the fan, not its edge.

**The reconstruction's null result.** The same discipline, applied to the council level, exonerates: no signal genuinely published in 2016–18 predicted *which* councils would fail (chance-level discrimination; councils' timeliness rank order reshuffled completely, ρ = 0.01 — Norfolk went from 9% of plans on time to 49%, Leicestershire from 98% to 5%). "You should have intervened in Devon in 2017" is a false charge, and any early-warning design built on ranking councils would have pointed at the wrong ones. The failure was national; so was the only available remedy. A post-mortem method that could not have produced this exonerating finding would not deserve trust when it convicts.

**The decision standard.** By mid-2017 the evidence supported every low-regret action: expanding the educational psychologist training intake (3–4 year pipeline, so a 2018 cohort arrives exactly when the assessment system later broke), a national capacity audit, plan quality standards, and the stress test itself. By 2018 — transition complete, spend trend confirmed in a third S251 outturn — it supported the capital tier: resourced provision and special-school places, landing 2022–24.

**The ledger and the cost.** What actually happened: a £250m patch in December 2018 (nineteen months after the 36σ breach), demand-following revenue from 2019, an accounting override in 2020, deficit-management deals from 2021 — and the supply-side responses last: the first major capital programme in **March 2022**, the real EP expansion in **March 2023**, its capacity arriving 2027, a decade after the signal. The counterfactual — the 2022 capital programme decided in mid-2018 — yields avoidable independent-placement spend of **~£340m** on the conservative floor and **~£1.24bn** on the central case through FY2025, still compounding at £145–477m for each further year, plus roughly **26,600 children's plans** issued late in 2022–24 that would have been on time had throughput merely held at its 2019 level. For scale: the entire low-regret package of 2017 — training places, an audit, standards — would have cost low tens of millions.

**The tripwire that never existed.** Every input to this post-mortem was published, on schedule, by the department itself, throughout. The failure was not data. It was that the reform's planning assumption had no published tripwire attached, so its breach — at 12σ, then 36σ, then 86σ — was nobody's defined problem. That is the precise defect the fourth discipline exists to remove, and the government's current SEND white paper, which rests on its own demand assumptions for a new four-tier system, is the natural first client.

## Objections

**"This is hindsight bias in a spreadsheet."** The method's two central disciplines exist to prevent exactly that: the publication-vintage rule bars the analyst from using anything unavailable at the time (and in the demonstration, disqualified an entire class of seemingly available signals), and the decision standard judges choices against ex-ante thresholds, not outcomes. The strongest evidence that the method is not hindsight-driven is that it returns null and exculpatory findings where the data requires them — mid-2016 ambiguity credited, council-level prediction ruled impossible.

**"It will become a blame machine and chill honest advice."** The AAIB has investigated every British air accident for a century without prosecuting pilots into silence, because its statutory purpose excludes blame; HSSIB extends the same protection to clinicians. The policy post-mortem inherits that design: its findings attach to mechanisms — an assumption without a tripwire, a monitoring gap, a sequencing error — not to individuals. The counterpart is candour: the post-mortem's inputs are published statistics, so it requires no testimony to convict a process.

**"We already do this — evaluations, reviews, scrutiny."** The Magenta Book evaluates *interventions*: did the programme work? Post-legislative scrutiny reviews *statutes*, thinly and late. Neither asks whether the *planning assumptions* under a statutory system are holding, on a clock fast enough to matter. The SEND record is the empirical rebuttal to "we already do this": every mechanism now cited existed throughout 2016–2022, and the breach ran for five years unactioned.

**"Every failure is unique; a standard method won't generalise."** The four questions are invariant precisely because they attach to structure, not subject: any system with statutory demand, published statistics, planning assumptions, and long supply lead times can breach in the same shape. Adult social care, temporary accommodation, court backlogs, prison places, and NHS dentistry all fit the template, and several are mid-breach now. The method's cost is a rounding error against any one of them.

## The ask

Three commitments, none requiring legislation to begin:

1. **Publish assumptions with tripwires.** Every major demand-led programme states, at launch, the demand and cost assumptions its funding rests on, and the deviation-duration-response triples attached to each. The SEND white paper should go first.
2. **Publish an annual assumptions-versus-outturn statement** across the demand-led state — the OBR discipline, extended from the macro forecast to the systems where assumption failure becomes crisis.
3. **Run one post-mortem to prove the method.** SEND is the candidate: the data is published, the method is demonstrated, the code is open, and the exercise above was completed by one analyst. A department, the NAO, or a select committee could commission the official version in months, not years.

Aviation did not become safe because pilots stopped making mistakes. It became safe because every failure was investigated quickly, blamelessly, and in public, and because the findings changed the system before the next flight. Government runs statutory systems whose failures unfold in its own published statistics, in slow motion, over years. It has every component of the discipline that would catch them — a statutory forecast evaluator, a blameless investigator, a quantitative auditor, a scrutiny calendar. It has never assembled them. The children who waited past every legal deadline for support that a 2017 decision would have funded are the cost of that gap. The next system is breaching its assumptions now.
