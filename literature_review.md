# Existing literature on the SEND crisis in England: a working record

*Compiled May 2026 — for context and positioning of the la-send-analysis work*

---

## What has already been written, and by whom

### Official and parliamentary bodies

**National Audit Office** (October 2024): *Support for Children and Young People with Special Educational Needs.* Found the system "not delivering better outcomes, financially unsustainable and in urgent need of reform." High-needs funding rose 58% over a decade to £10.7bn in 2024/25; LA deficits could reach £4.6bn by March 2026; 43% of LAs face potential Section 114 notices. The most comprehensive official statement of the financial crisis. Does not model which LAs were at risk earlier.

**Public Accounts Committee** (2024, HC 353): Parallel inquiry to the NAO. Corroborated the financial sustainability narrative and pressed on private equity involvement in independent special schools (>30% backed by PE firms).

**Education Select Committee** (October 2024): *Solving the SEND Crisis* — 95 recommendations from 12 oral evidence sessions and 700+ written submissions. Calls to strengthen SEND accountability in Ofsted, ring-fence post-16 funding. A follow-on inquiry ran through late 2025, accepting written evidence.

**House of Lords Select Committee on the Children and Families Act 2014** (December 2022): Concluded the Act had "largely missed" its opportunity; government departments made "no meaningful effort" to evaluate its impact. Explicitly found the government was "flying blind on data." The closest official acknowledgement that monitoring failures contributed to the crisis.

**Ofsted/CQC Joint Area SEND Inspections** (new framework from 2023): 26 inspections in 2023; 8 found "widespread and systemic failings." The East Midlands was worst: 4 of 5 inspected LAs systemic. LA-specific findings not systematically modelled in the public literature.

**DfE — 2023 SEND and AP Improvement Plan** (March 2023, previous government): Three-outcome framework; introduced national/local inclusion dashboards, standardised EHCP template, new inspection framework. Widely regarded — including by the current government — as insufficient. The 2026 consultation does not reference it; current administration narrative is that structural redesign was required.

**DfE — *Every Child Achieving and Thriving* white paper** (February 2026): Most significant redesign since 2014. Four-tier support system; EHCPs restricted to most complex needs from 2030; Individual Support Plans for lower-need children (without tribunal rights); £7bn investment; national price bands for independent special school fees; High Needs Stability Grant covering 90% of eligible LA deficits.

### Think tanks

**Institute for Fiscal Studies** (December 2024): *Spending on Special Educational Needs in England.* Key quantitative findings:
- Independent placement costs up 138% since 2015 (£1.8bn in 2024)
- OBR-forecast £6bn gap between expected funding and spend by 2028
- Each additional 1ppt of EHCP pupils in independent provision linked to 1.2ppt larger schools deficit (regression result)
- ASD pupils in London are 16ppt more likely to have an EHCP than those in the West Midlands, controlling for need type — LA variation persists beyond need composition

This is the most methodologically sophisticated cross-sectional analysis in the public domain. It is cross-sectional (one regression) not predictive.

**Institute for Government — Performance Tracker 2025: Schools** (November 2025, Amber Dellar): LA-level analysis with some regression. Key findings:
- ASD now 34% of all EHCPs (149,200 plans), up from 26% (57,500) in 2016
- Girls' ASD identification up 335%, boys up 126% since 2016 (diagnostic catch-up)
- Per-EHCP support halved in real terms: ~£29,000 in 2015/16 to £14,700 in 2024/25
- Less deprived LAs have *larger* deficits (12.3% of schools funding in least-deprived fifth vs. 1.4% in most-deprived) — driven by independent placement preference, not deprivation-driven demand
- State special schools operating at 104% capacity nationally

The most detailed publicly available LA-level quantitative work. Descriptive and cross-sectional; does not produce risk scores or predictive models.

**Education Policy Institute** (April 2019, Natalie Perera): *High Needs Funding: An Overview of the Key Issues.* Documented ~£470m cumulative LA deficit by 2018/19 and structural rigidity of the funding formula. Functions as an early warning document from 2019. A follow-on EPI report (2024, Nuffield Foundation-funded, *Identifying SEND*) focuses on identification inequalities across LAs; notes LA variation persists even controlling for need type.

**Cordis Bright** — *SEN Stress-Test* (published since 2014; most recent July/August 2025, covering 2024 data): Annual descriptive benchmarking using 7 indicators to rank LA stress. Well-regarded as a monitoring tool. Explicitly descriptive; not predictive modelling.

**iMPOWER** — *IMPOWER Index*: Annual LA benchmarking on outcomes per pound of high-needs spend. Also won the DfE Delivering Better Value (DBV) contract; reviewed ~700 EHCP case files across DBV LAs and found 2/3 of plans "not personalised." DBV is associated with the Lincolnshire case (414 fewer EHCPs framed as "cost avoidance"), which became a cause célèbre for gatekeeping by policy rather than need.

### Academic literature

**Daniels, Thompson & Tawell (2019)** — "After Warnock: The Effects of Perverse Incentives," *Frontiers in Education.* Argues the 2014 Act created structural contradictions between inclusion policy and school accountability incentives; removal of "School Action" categories acted as an unintended gatekeeping mechanism. A frequently-cited theoretical account of why the Act's design produced perverse outcomes.

**Azpitarte et al. (2024)** — "Failing children with SEND in England," *British Educational Research Journal.* LA-level quantitative analysis of SEN outcomes at Key Stage 1. Outcome analysis, not system failure prediction.

**Marsh, Alan (March 2026)** — "The English SEND Tribunal Appeal Rate: Moving the Goalposts to Sustainability?" (BERA blog). Most rigorous standalone analysis of tribunal data 2014–2025: 8x growth in appeals (3,147 to 25,002); 83% surge in the most recent 24 months; parent win rate 95–99%; southeast 4.3% vs. northwest 2.0% appeal rate (inversely correlated with deprivation — wealthier families drive tribunal use). Critically documents the **2018 DfE methodology change** that switched the denominator from all EHCPs to "appealable decisions," reducing the headline rate from 24.7 to 3.2 per 10,000. Marsh argues this was a political decision to make the rate appear manageable. *Implication for the forecastability analysis: the published tribunal rate series contains a step-change break in 2018 that partly reflects methodology, not just genuine improvement. Any analysis using pre- and post-2018 tribunal data should account for this.*

**Russell et al. (2022)** — "Time trends in autism diagnosis over 20 years," *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry.* Found a 787% increase in diagnosed autism in GP data 1998–2018; notes population-based estimates remain broadly stable — the growth reflects broadening diagnostic criteria and improved recognition, not a true prevalence change. Key reference for the ASD demand story: the system absorbed a genuine surge in diagnoses, but this was a recognition surge, not (primarily) an epidemiological one.

### Journalism and advocacy

**Schools Week**: Lead publication for investigative SEND journalism. LA-by-LA timeliness tracking; Safety Valve criticism; the iMPOWER-Lincolnshire story; private equity in special schools. DfE confirmation (December 2024) of no further Safety Valve agreements reported here first.

**Special Needs Jungle (SNJ)**: Annual "LA Hall of Shame" on timeliness. "55% rise in 2024 tribunal appeals. LAs' 1.3% success rate cost £153m." Detailed Safety Valve impact analysis.

**IPSEA** (Independent Provider of Special Education Advice): Tribunal statistics analysis and legal rights documentation. States 96–99% parent win rate at tribunal (note: includes consent orders — settlements made just before hearing; this number reflects LA capitulation as well as unlawful initial decisions).

**Contact** (charity for families with disabled children): *Nearly all parents forced to tribunal win.* Campaigned on the £59.8m wasted on failed LA tribunal defences.

**N8 Research Partnership / Child of the North** (October 2025): *Addressing the SEND Assessment and Support Crisis.* Lancaster University researchers. 46.6% EHCP issuance within 20 weeks; 92% of families report the system is detrimental to their mental health.

## What the existing literature has and has not done

### Well-documented in the literature

- Timeliness failure trajectory (documented rigorously by all major reports)
- Independent placement cost spiral (IFS, IfG, NAO, PAC, ITV)
- DSG deficit problem and LA variation in placement rates (IFS, IfG)
- ASD/SEMH diagnosis growth as driver of demand (Russell et al., IfG Performance Tracker)
- Tribunal win rates for parents and rising appeal volumes (Marsh 2026, IPSEA, SNJ)
- Gatekeeping vs. capacity collapse debate (extensively contested)
- 2014 Act design flaws and perverse incentives (Daniels et al. 2019, House of Lords 2022)

### Not done in the existing literature

The following are not found in any published work identified in this sweep:

1. **Predictive modelling of LA-level SEND system failure with model validation.** No published work applies statistical modelling with cross-validation to predict which LAs would enter timeliness, legal-pressure, or placement failure. The Cordis Bright stress-test and iMPOWER Index are descriptive benchmarks. The IFS runs one cross-sectional regression. The IfG does LA-level descriptive comparisons. None use leave-one-out cross-validation or test multiple model specifications against held-out outcomes.

2. **The two-failure-mode distinction.** No published analysis distinguishes timeliness failure from legal-pressure/placement failure as *separate phenomena predicted by different leading indicators*. The literature treats "the SEND crisis" as one thing.

3. **The absolute count vs. share distinction as a monitoring design point.** The IFS and IfG both track ASD as a *share* of EHCPs. The finding that shares are near-useless for prediction while absolute counts are informative is a direct critique of how official monitoring is designed — and it does not appear in the literature.

4. **LA-level risk scores grounded in a validated predictive model.** Cordis Bright ranks LAs descriptively; no published work generates forward-looking risk scores from a validated model.

5. **The stress-testing vs. forecasting distinction as a policy accountability framing.** No existing analysis frames the policy failure as a failure to stress-test rather than a failure to forecast. The House of Lords finding that government was "flying blind" is the closest, but it doesn't draw the distinction.

6. **The lead-time table and "window for low-regret action."** No existing work quantifies when signals became visible alongside the lead times for the relevant interventions — the argument that "wait until certain = do nothing" for 4-year-lead actions.

## Key counter-arguments to anticipate

**The structural redesign argument.** The government's primary accountability narrative (2026 white paper) is that the 2014 Act's design was the root cause — structural redesign was required, not earlier management of the existing system. The implication is that the low-regret interventions identified in this analysis would have been insufficient without legislative change. This argument is partly correct: structural reform *was also* needed. But the low-regret interventions (EP training, quality standards, capacity audit, resourced provision units) are complementary to any reform path, not alternatives to it. They would have reduced the harm within the old system while a redesign was developed. The argument for acting on them does not depend on the old system being permanently viable.

**The unforeseeable ASD surge.** Some pushback will invoke Russell et al. (2022) — even academic demographers were surprised by the speed and scale of the recognition surge. The counter is already embedded in the stress-testing framing: the government didn't need to predict autism prevalence, it needed to test whether the system survived plausible demand scenarios.

**"We did act" (Safety Valve / DBV).** DfE's own December 2024 admission that Safety Valve "had not been effective enough" weakens this rebuttal, but expect it to be deployed. The relevant response is that both programmes were *reactive* (targeting councils already in financial distress) rather than *early-warning* responses to the signals visible from 2016 onward.

**The tribunal methodology break.** The 2018 DfE methodology change (Marsh 2026) means the pre-2018 tribunal rate is not directly comparable to post-2018. This is relevant to any analysis that uses tribunal data across this period — worth a footnote or caveat in the forecastability methodology.
