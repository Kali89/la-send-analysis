# Policy asks
### What the data says should happen

---

These asks are ordered by lead time. The capital decisions must come first precisely because they take longest to deliver.

---

## 1. Publish a national SEND provision investment plan

The problem is structural and geographically concentrated. England does not have a national picture of where maintained SEN capacity — by need type, by locality — is mismatched against current demand and projected growth. Without that picture, every commissioning decision is reactive.

**Ask**: DfE should publish a national LA × need-type capital plan showing where England needs new maintained special schools, resourced provision programmes, and SEN unit expansions; the gap against projected 2030 demand; expected lead times; and delivery milestones. This analysis provides a data-led starting point: a ranked list of 467 LA × need-type combinations ordered by designation mismatch, demand growth, independent placement pressure, and geographic access.

---

## 2. Commit the capital — treat provision as an invest-to-save programme

Independent special school placements cost a median of £97,322 per child per year in 2023/24 (S251 outturn). Maintained special school places cost approximately £22,000. The difference — not a gap in education quality, but a gap in local provision — flows directly into DSG deficits.

A modelled top-30 programme of new maintained facilities would require approximately £420 million of capital investment. On conservative assumptions (£75,000 saving per child diverted from independent to maintained, 40% diversion rate), this generates estimated annual savings of £76 million at full operation and a Green Book-style 15-year discounted net present value of £220 million. The portfolio reaches undiscounted break-even in 2034 — nine years after capital commitment. Every year of delay locks in further independent placement costs at current rates while making future savings arrive later.

**Ask**: DfE and HM Treasury should treat maintained SEN capacity expansion as a capital invest-to-save programme. The fiscal case is positive; the question is whether the capital decision is made in time to matter.

---

## 3. Stress-test the reform against lead times before implementation

The 2026 SEND white paper is the right long-run response. But structural reform and immediate operational intervention are not alternatives — they operate on different timescales. New maintained special schools take four to six years from planning to first pupils. A decision committed in 2027 delivers no new places before 2033.

**Ask**: DfE should publish, alongside any reform framework, an explicit lead-time stress test: how many children will be assessed, wait, and be placed in independent provision in each year between now and when new maintained capacity becomes available? The answer makes the cost of delay concrete and forces an honest conversation about the gap between reform ambition and operational reality.

---

## 4. Publish a public early-warning dashboard

The data to predict local authority collapse already exists and is published annually. Predictive models trained on 2016 data identified the councils that would enter the Safety Valve programme — achieving AUC 0.70 six years in advance, and AUC 0.88 by 2021. DfE's own monitoring has focused on proportional need-type shares, which perform at AUC ≈ 0.50 (equivalent to chance). The warning signals — absolute need-type growth, 20-week timeliness trend, tribunal appeal rate, independent placement spend as a share of DSG — were visible and were not acted on.

**Ask**: DfE should publish an annual LA-level risk dashboard using the validated early-warning signals identified in this analysis. The methodology and underlying data should be transparent and open to external scrutiny.

---

## 5. Publish LA-level need-type data and school-level specialism data annually

Two data gaps currently prevent effective local commissioning. First, LA-level EHCP counts by primary need type (ASD, SEMH, SLCN, MLD) are missing for 2020/21–2023/24 — four years of data unavailable for trend analysis. Second, maintained special school-level specialism and registered capacity data are not published in a form that allows systematic need-type matching against demand.

**Ask**: DfE should publish LA-level need-type breakdowns annually as part of the SEN2 statistical release, and publish school-level SEN specialism and capacity data in a structured, machine-readable format.

---

## 6. Confirm EP workforce expansion is on track

Educational psychologist services are a bottleneck at every stage of the EHCP process. Safety Valve councils already spend 41% more per EHCP on administration — the signature of backlogs, not efficiency. Training an EP takes three to four years after a psychology degree. The expansion programmes in the 2023 SEND Improvement Plan are the right response, but the pipeline is long.

**Ask**: DfE should confirm that EP training place expansions are on track and publish LA-level workforce projection data showing when additional capacity will reach the frontline.

---

## 7. Proactively engage the six councils now showing pre-crisis signatures

Bristol, Birmingham, Bromley, Lewisham, Staffordshire, and Central Bedfordshire show the same structural signatures — timeliness failure, rising independent placement pressure, tribunal challenge rates — as councils that subsequently entered the Safety Valve programme. Several are already in de facto collapse on the 20-week statutory standard. The Safety Valve programme was designed for exactly this situation.

**Ask**: DfE should confirm whether it is already engaging these councils, and on what basis. If not, proactive engagement should begin immediately — while there is still time to intervene before deficits become unmanageable.
