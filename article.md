# England's SEND System Has a System-Wide Timeliness Problem — and the Data Show Why

**Half of all councils are failing the 20-week legal deadline. The cause is not which DfE programme a council is in. It is a structural mismatch between what the maintained sector provides and what children now need.**

*Analysis of DfE SEN2 2025 data and GIAS school capacity data | May 2026*

---

Every year, tens of thousands of families in England apply to their local council for an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP) — the legal document that unlocks specialist support for children with special educational needs. There is a legal 20-week deadline for councils to complete the assessment and issue a plan. In 2024, more than half of councils in England were missing it for the majority of the children they assessed.

This is not a story about which DfE intervention programme a council belongs to. It is a story about a system-wide structural failure — and the data now available are detailed enough to explain what is driving it.

---

## The scale of the timeliness failure

The analysis covers 151 upper-tier local authorities in England, using the DfE SEN2 2025 statistical release.

In 2024, the national average for plans issued within the 20-week legal limit was **51.8%**. That means nearly half of all children who were assessed and found to need an EHCP waited longer than the law allows. The distribution across councils is wide and severe: **59 of 151 councils (39%) issued fewer than 40% of plans on time**. Twenty-four councils issued fewer than 20% of plans on time.

The worst performers span every category of council — those in DfE Safety Valve agreements, those in Delivering Better Value, and those in no programme at all:

| Local authority | Programme | 20-week compliance |
|---|---|---|
| Devon | Safety Valve | 3.2% |
| Portsmouth | None | 4.3% |
| Leicestershire | DBV | 4.3% |
| Kingston upon Hull | None | 5.8% |
| Plymouth | None | 6.0% |
| Redbridge | None | 7.0% |
| Cornwall | DBV | 7.4% |
| Slough | Safety Valve | 7.4% |
| Cambridgeshire | Safety Valve | 7.7% |
| Stockport | DBV | 7.8% |

Devon issued plans on time in **3 out of every 100 cases**. Portsmouth, with no DfE programme involvement, was barely better. This is not a phenomenon confined to financially distressed councils in DfE agreements. It is a system-wide operational failure.

### What the comparison between council types shows — and does not show

The DfE has been running two intervention programmes for local authorities with large SEND high-needs budget deficits: Safety Valve (SV) and Delivering Better Value (DBV). It is natural to ask whether councils in these programmes perform worse on timeliness.

The current data do not support a clear programme-effect story. Safety Valve councils average 53.2% timeliness; councils in no programme average 52.3%. The difference is not statistically significant (Mann-Whitney p = 0.904). While some of the most severely failing councils are in Safety Valve agreements, others are not, and the variation within each group is large.

The more informative question is not which programme a council belongs to, but what structural factors predict poor performance across all councils.

---

## How long are children actually waiting?

The SEN2 2025 data breaks delays into three bands for the first time: within 20 weeks, between 20 weeks and one year, and over one year.

Nationally in 2024, **51.8%** of EHCPs were issued within the legal limit, **40.9%** took between 20 weeks and a year, and **7.3%** took longer than a year. The bulk of the delay falls in the middle bucket — children waiting six months to a year, not the multi-year waits that tend to generate headlines. But the scale is large: in a typical struggling council, more than half of all children received their plan in the 20-week-to-one-year band, arriving between five months and a year after the original request, against a legal deadline of 20 weeks.

The worst individual cases of plans taking over one year are distributed across all programme categories. Leeds (57.2% of plans took over a year, Delivering Better Value) and Kirklees (46.2%, Delivering Better Value) are the most extreme cases nationally; Leicestershire (44.6%, no intervention) is third. The timeliness crisis is widespread, and the very longest individual delays cut across all council types.

## The gatekeeping question

Alongside the timeliness failure, there is a widespread belief among SEND advocates that councils under financial pressure are quietly refusing more applications to keep costs down.

The data do not support this picture — at least not for systematic differences between programme categories.

In 2024, Safety Valve councils refused an average of **26.7%** of EHCP assessment requests. DBV councils refused 24.6%. Councils in no programme refused 22.9%. The difference between Safety Valve and no-programme councils is not statistically significant (Mann-Whitney p = 0.126).

The highest refusal rates in 2024 span all categories: Walsall (DBV, 60.6%), Sunderland (DBV, 59.3%), Kent (Safety Valve, 55.0%), East Sussex (Safety Valve, 53.7%), Southwark (no intervention, 47.7%). Refusal rates are variable and high across the board; they are not a reliable marker of which type of programme a council is in.

This does not mean refusal decisions are always appropriate. It means the headline refusal rate does not cleanly differentiate financially distressed councils from others. The families waiting more than 20 weeks in Devon, Portsmouth, and Leicestershire are not being refused — they are waiting. And what explains those waits requires looking further into the system.

![Refusal rates by local authority and intervention status, 2024](outputs/figures/02_la_refusal_rates_2024.png)

## The mechanism: provision mismatch, not gatekeeping

The most consistent finding across the analysis is structural, and it applies across all council types. Councils are failing on timeliness because the maintained sector was built to serve a population with different needs from the one that now holds EHCPs — and the gap between what the maintained sector provides and what children need is being filled expensively, and unevenly, through independent specialist placements.

### A decade of shifting need

The national EHCP need profile has shifted substantially over the past decade:

| Need type | 2015/16 | 2024/25 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Autistic Spectrum Disorder | 25.8% | 31.5% | +5.7pp |
| Social, Emotional and Mental Health | 12.7% | 20.7% | +8.0pp |
| Moderate Learning Difficulty | 13.4% | 10.6% | −2.8pp |
| Speech, Language and Communication | 13.9% | 21.3% | +7.4pp |

Social, Emotional and Mental Health (SEMH) as a share of all EHCP children has grown by **8 percentage points in ten years** — from roughly 1 in 8 to 1 in 5. ASD has grown by nearly 6 points. The population of children with EHCPs has changed substantially.

The maintained school estate has not kept pace. New maintained special school openings have moved in the wrong direction. Among maintained special schools with known opening dates, **SEMH schools accounted for 22% of new openings before 2016**. Since 2016, that figure has fallen to **11%**. ASD-specialist schools have held broadly steady as a share of new openings (19.9% pre-2016, 18.2% post-2016). The maintained sector is building capacity in line with pre-existing provision profiles — not shifting toward where need has grown fastest.

The result is a structural gap that is now universal. Across all local authorities with complete data, the share of EHCP children with SEMH needs exceeds the share of maintained special school capacity designated for SEMH by a **median of 13 percentage points nationally**. The ASD gap is similar in magnitude. This mismatch is a national feature of the maintained sector, not a characteristic of any particular group of councils.

### Access gaps, not geography

The provision mismatch matters most where the provision that does exist is hardest to reach. Using population-weighted centroids for all 33,755 Lower Layer Super Output Areas (LSOAs) in England, matched to the location of every maintained special school by primary SEN type, it is possible to compute mean distances from each LA's population to its nearest maintained SEMH or ASD school.

When these distance measures enter a regression predicting independent placement top-up spend per EHCP (S251 line 1.2.3, 2023/24), one finding is significant and one is a clear null.

The **mean distance to the nearest maintained SEMH school is a significant positive predictor of independent spend per EHCP** (β = +0.016 per km, p = 0.003, n = 146): councils whose populations live further from state SEMH provision spend more on independent placements, after controlling for need-type profile, deprivation, EHCP prevalence, and region.

The null is equally informative: **geographic population dispersal — how spread-out an LA's population is relative to its own centroid — has no predictive value** (p = 0.84). A sprawling shire county does not spend more on independent placements than a compact urban authority, once the actual provision distance is controlled for. Dispersal matters only insofar as it creates provision gaps; it does not independently drive cost. The expensive councils are not expensive because they are large — they are expensive because SEMH provision is thin relative to the need profile of their population.

This model, using structural access and demographic factors, explains **27% of the variance** in independent placement spend across councils (R² = 0.27, n = 146 with complete data). The remaining 73% is unexplained by these structural factors alone — almost certainly reflecting historical commissioning patterns, tribunal behaviour, and local authority-specific decisions that cross-sectional data cannot capture.

![LSOA population-weighted distance to nearest maintained special school by intervention status](outputs/figures/26_lsoa_distance_by_status.png)

### Independent supply is the stronger predictor

When independent special school capacity per pupil — rather than maintained — enters the regression, a different and stronger relationship emerges. Across 135 LAs with complete data, independent special school capacity per 1,000 pupils explains **22.5% of the variance** in independent placement rates (β = 0.197, p < 0.001). By contrast, deprivation (IMD) alone explains less than 1% of the variance in placement rates (R² = 0.009, p = 0.26). When both variables enter the same regression, the IMD coefficient collapses to zero (p = 0.87) — it is entirely absorbed by independent supply.

This is the strongest statistical predictor of independent placement rates in the dataset. Areas with more independent special school places nearby have more independent placements, and the relationship holds even after controlling for deprivation.

What this finding cannot tell us is which direction the causal arrow runs. Did independent schools establish themselves in areas because existing demand was already there, or does their presence subsequently generate placements? The cross-sectional data cannot resolve this. Resolving it would require historical data on when independent schools opened relative to when placement rates rose — data not currently published.

![Regional capacity profile and independent placement rates](outputs/figures/12_regional_capacity_profile.png)

### What the supply data cannot tell us

The GIAS capacity and utilisation figures capture whether a place physically exists and whether it is occupied. They do not capture whether it is the *right kind* of place. A council may have abundant maintained special school capacity for children with moderate learning difficulties but almost none for non-verbal autism or severe SEMH needs — precisely the categories driving EHCP growth nationally.

GIAS registered capacity is also the DfE's administrative figure; it is not updated in real time to reflect a school's actual ability to take on another child with complex, high-cost needs. A school that nominally has available places may be operationally at capacity given current staffing ratios.

## What spending data reveals about capacity

The S251 LA expenditure returns allow a partial picture of what has happened to SEND assessment capacity over time.

Nationally, spending on SEN administration per EHCP has risen from a median of **£448 in 2019 to £571 in 2024** — a 27% real increase. EP spend has been roughly flat in nominal terms over the same period (£378 to £395), meaning it has fallen in real terms as EHCP numbers have grown. This is the spending signature of a service that is absorbing more administrative overhead to manage growing backlogs while EP capacity has not kept pace.

Among councils in DfE programmes, the pattern shows differences consistent with varying operational pressure. Safety Valve councils spend a median of **£716 per EHCP on administration**, compared to **£507** in Delivering Better Value authorities. This likely reflects the overhead of managing large caseload backlogs — more correspondence, more review meetings, more chasing — not more effective service delivery. Transport spend per EHCP is also higher in Safety Valve authorities (median **£2,909** vs **£2,276**), consistent with higher independent specialist school placement rates, which tend to be further from home than maintained alternatives.

On educational psychologist spend per EHCP issued — the most direct proxy available for assessment capacity — Safety Valve councils spend a median of **£364 per EHCP** in 2024. This is consistent with EP workforce shortage as a constraint: fewer EPs processing more cases generates lower apparent spend-per-plan, not because councils are investing more efficiently but because the bottleneck limits throughput.

## Were struggling councils already struggling before DfE intervention?

A critical question for interpreting these findings is whether DfE intervention programmes identify councils that were already deteriorating, or whether the programmes themselves have effects — positive or negative — on outcomes.

The event study analysis — comparing councils entering Safety Valve agreements against controls in the years before programme entry — provides a clear answer on timeliness: **Safety Valve LAs were already 9.1 percentage points worse on 20-week compliance in the three years before they entered the programme**.

The tribunal gap pre-entry was much smaller (+0.1 percentage points). Tribunal appeal rates were generally low across all LAs in the 2014–2019 period; the widening of the gap in more recent years appears to be driven by the general worsening of timeliness and plan quality rather than a programme-specific effect.

This supports the interpretation that Safety Valve status largely captures pre-existing structural weakness — councils that had been underfunding their SEND provision for years before the crisis became visible in their budget. The programme did not create the problem; it identified it. Whether it has reversed it is a different question.

## What we still cannot explain

The analysis identifies what is happening with confidence: widespread timeliness failure cutting across all council types, driven by structural mismatches between maintained provision and need profiles, with access distance to SEMH schools a significant predictor of independent placement cost. What the data cannot yet fully explain is which councils' outcomes are most determined by each mechanism.

Three hypotheses remain plausible and are not mutually exclusive:

**1. Wrong type of provision.** The need-type and supply analysis above confirms this is a real and universal problem: SEMH need has grown by 8 percentage points since 2015/16 while new maintained SEMH school openings have halved as a share of the new-build programme. The LSOA access analysis reinforces it: SEMH distance is a significant predictor of independent spend. But the mismatch is too uniform across council types to explain which specific LAs spend the most — what varies is geographic access to the SEMH provision that does exist.

**2. Tribunal behaviour.** Families who successfully argue at tribunal for a named independent placement create a placement the council must fund. The SEND Tribunal upholds parental choice in roughly 80% of cases. Whether particular councils lose tribunal cases at higher rates — and whether those losses explain a disproportionate share of their independent placements — requires tribunal outcome data broken down by LA and placement type, which is not currently published.

**3. Historical commissioning lock-in.** Some councils may have built long-standing relationships with specific independent providers and continue to use them as default placements, even where maintained alternatives have since developed. This would require historical placement data not available in the published SEN2 series.

The most policy-actionable of these hypotheses is the first. If the problem is a mismatch between the type of maintained provision available and the type of need driving placements, building more of the same kind of maintained school will not fix it. The question is whether the *right specialism* exists in the maintained sector — and that requires a different dataset.

## The full picture: a causal chain

The analytical threads in this piece converge on a single account. It is worth stating it plainly before drawing conclusions.

**Over the past decade, the population of children with EHCPs changed.** Social, Emotional and Mental Health needs grew from 13% to 21% of all EHCP children between 2015/16 and 2024/25 — an 8 percentage-point shift. Autistic Spectrum Disorder grew from 26% to 32%. These are the two most contested, most expensive-to-place categories, and the most likely to end in tribunal when the provision offered is unsuitable.

**The maintained school estate did not follow.** SEMH schools fell from 22% to 11% of new maintained special school openings over the same period. Places grew, but in the wrong proportions. This mismatch is not a South East problem — it is national, running at a median of 13 percentage points across all council types.

**What makes some councils expensive is access, not supply volume.** The maintained SEMH provision that does exist is unevenly accessible. Councils whose populations live further from their nearest maintained SEMH school spend significantly more on independent placements (β = +0.016 per km, p = 0.003, n = 146). Large shire counties — particularly those in the South East — tend to have SEMH schools concentrated in county towns, leaving rural populations far from any state alternative. Geographic size alone is not the mechanism: how spread-out a council's population is has no independent predictive value (p = 0.84) once actual provision distance is measured.

**The cost consequence is independent specialist placements.** At £60,000–120,000 per place per year, these placements are almost certainly the primary driver of DSG deficits in the worst-affected authorities. Where councils have more independent special school places nearby, placement rates are higher (R² = 0.225, p < 0.001). Where maintained SEMH provision is distant, the gap is filled at independent sector prices.

**Financial pressure feeds back into operational failure.** DSG deficits constrain staffing, which constrains EP workforce capacity, which limits the number of assessments completed within 20 weeks. The spending data tells this story: national SEN administration spend per EHCP has risen 27% in real terms since 2019, while EP spend per plan has been flat in nominal terms — not reflecting efficiency but the bottleneck on throughput. The result is growing backlogs, thin or rushed plans when they finally arrive, and families challenging the quality at tribunal.

**The timeliness failure is universal and predates any single programme.** In 2024, 59 of 151 councils issued fewer than 40% of plans on time. Twenty-four issued fewer than 20%. The bottom of the table includes councils from every programme category and none. Councils entering Safety Valve agreements were already 9.1 percentage points worse on timeliness before they entered — the programme identified a problem that was already there.

**The headline metric most commonly scrutinised shows limited signal.** EHCP refusal rates in 2024 range from Safety Valve (26.7%) to DBV (24.6%) to no programme (22.9%), but the differences are not statistically significant and the highest individual refusal rates are spread across all categories. The families failing to receive timely plans are not primarily being refused at the front door. They are waiting behind it.

The crisis in England's SEND system is not a gatekeeping crisis caused by any particular programme or group of councils. It is the expression of a structural mismatch between what the maintained special school system was built to provide and what the EHCP population now needs — playing out through the finances of councils across the country that have been funding the gap with expensive independent placements, and whose budgets, staffing, and assessment capacity have been hollowing out as a result.

## What needs to happen

The policy implications of this reading differ from those of the gatekeeping hypothesis.

If the problem is gatekeeping, the solution is scrutiny of refusal decisions. But if the problem is structural provision mismatch combined with an expensive placement burden, scrutiny of refusal rates is largely beside the point. It keeps the front door open while the system behind it is overwhelmed, and does nothing about the cost driver.

The more productive policy agenda is commissioning-led. Councils and the DfE need to know — LA by LA — which need types are growing fastest, which maintained schools serve those needs, how far the population is from them, and how that gap compares to independent sector availability. That is a data infrastructure problem, and it is largely solvable with data already collected.

Four improvements to published data would substantially strengthen future analysis and commissioning decisions:

1. **School-level SEN specialism data** — what types of needs each special school caters to, matched to LA EHCP need profiles. Would allow councils to test the "wrong type" hypothesis directly and plan capacity in response to their own need-type trends.
2. **SEND team staffing data by LA** — DfE holds this through the School Workforce Census but does not publish it at LA level in usable form. The single most important missing variable for understanding timeliness failures. S251 spending data (used here) is a proxy, but staffing headcount would be far more direct.
3. **Tribunal outcome data by LA and placement type** — would quantify how much of the independent placement burden is tribunal-driven versus council-agreed, and where tribunal losses are most concentrated.
4. **LA-level SEND legal costs** — not currently published; invisible in financial data but essential for testing whether the tribunal cost spiral is the key feedback mechanism in DSG deficit growth.

Better data does not fix provision gaps. But it is a precondition for commissioning decisions that respond to actual need profiles rather than inherited provision patterns.

## Data and methodology

All data used in this analysis are from official DfE or MHCLG sources:

- **DfE SEN2 2025 statistical release** — requests, timeliness, caseload, new plans (LA-level, 2019–2024); timeliness three-bucket breakdown (within 20w / 20w–1yr / >1yr) available for 2023–2024
- **SEND Tribunal appeal rate 2014–2024** — DfE supporting file, first published 2025
- **S251 LA and School Expenditure 2015/16–2024/25** — DSG carry-forward balance by LA; EP service (line 2.1.1), SEN administration (2.1.2), and SEN transport (2.1.4) expenditure by LA across ten years
- **SEN pupils 2024–25** — total pupils by LA for per-pupil denominators
- **IMD 2019** — average deprivation score by upper-tier LA (IoD2019, MHCLG)
- **GIAS (Get Information About Schools)** — full establishment file, May 2026; school type, capacity, and pupil numbers for all state-funded special schools
- **LSOA 2021 population-weighted centroids** — ONS ArcGIS (33,755 England LSOAs), joined to upper-tier LA via ONS LSOA21–UTLA22 lookup; per-LSOA distances computed by vectorised nearest-neighbour search (scipy cKDTree) against all maintained special schools by primary SEN type
- **SEN2 historical need-type data (2015/16–2019/20)** — DfE SEN2 2019–20 release, `sen_age_gender.csv`; LA-level EHCP pupils by primary need type for five academic years; joined to current 2024/25 data to form the ten-year demand trend

Intervention status (Safety Valve, Delivering Better Value) assigned from DfE programme announcements; 38 local authorities hold active agreements across both programmes. Non-parametric group tests use Mann-Whitney U and Kruskal-Wallis H. OLS regressions include IMD 2019 average score as a deprivation control and region fixed effects.

The full analysis code, data pipeline, and outputs are available at: **[github.com/Kali89/la-send-analysis](https://github.com/Kali89/la-send-analysis)**
